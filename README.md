# Health Score Dashboard

## Description

The Health Score Dashboard is designed to provide holistic, general, evidence-based guidance for living a well-balanced and healthy lifestyle. Its purpose is to synthesise widely accepted, institutional-level recommendations—such as government and public health guidance—into a single, practical tool. By focusing on the most impactful habits, the app helps users cut through influencer-driven trends and optimisation culture that overemphasise niche interventions and can create unnecessary stress. This tool aims to empower users to prioritise behaviours that reliably improve energy, resilience, physical function, mental wellness, and long-term health.

---

## Features

- **Four Key Categories:** Recovery, Nutrition, Activity, and Mental wellness, each with a weighted contribution to overall health.  
- **Self-Assessment Scoring:** Users assign scores to behaviours based on their adherence to guidelines.  
- **Weighted Calculations:** Automatic aggregation of individual factors into category scores and an overall health score.  
- **Evidence-Based Guidance:** Provides practical reference points for optimal and 80/20 levels for each behaviour.  
- **Interactive Tables:** Enter and update scores easily; all calculations update in real time.  
- **Tooltips & Commentary:** Concise explanations for each factor and category, highlighting why it matters.  

---

## How to Use

1. Download the `index.html` file to your computer.  
2. Ensure the file is saved with the `.html` extension.  
3. Double-click the file to open it in your default web browser.  
4. Navigate between the tabs (Recovery, Nutrition, Activity, Mental) and input self-scores to see weighted category and overall scores update in real time.

# In depth commentary

# Health Score Dashboard — Reference Guide

This document summarizes the health scoring framework: the categories, their weights, the "Optimal" vs "80/20" targets, and the explanatory info-tip commentary for each item.

## Overall Health Score

> **Info tip:** This summary blends your Recovery, Mental, Activity and Nutrition scores into a single view so you can quickly see where your current habits are strongest, where the biggest deficits are, and which changes are most likely to improve both day-to-day vitality and broader long-term health.

Use this page as your high-level summary. It is designed to cut through hype, highlight the most meaningful health drivers first, and show where practical changes are likely to matter most for both how you feel and how you are tracking overall.

| Category  | Weight |
|-----------|--------|
| Recovery  | 30%    |
| Mental    | 30%    |
| Activity  | 20%    |
| Nutrition | 20%    |

**Why these weights (category info tips):**
- **Recovery** — weighted highly because sleep appears to be one of the strongest drivers of survival, resilience and day-to-day wellbeing. It is not weighted even higher because health is still shaped by multiple interacting pillars, and consistent gains across several areas tend to outperform over-focusing on one.
- **Mental** — weighted highly because social connection is strongly linked to both longer-term survival and quality of life, even after accounting for other health factors. Its weight reflects that outsized role while still leaving room for the physical and behavioural pillars that support overall health alongside it.
- **Activity** — keeps a substantial weight because aerobic fitness, resistance training and baseline movement are consistently associated with major reductions in mortality risk and chronic disease. It sits below Recovery and Mental to reflect the especially strong survival signal seen for sleep and social connection, not because movement is a minor factor.
- **Nutrition** — remains meaningfully weighted because it still shapes metabolic health, body composition, disease risk and long-term outcomes, especially when diet quality is poor or ultra-processed foods are high. Its role here also reflects the value of making solid improvements across all pillars rather than chasing perfection in food alone.

## Recovery

> **Info tip:** Recovery covers the habits that allow the body and mind to restore function, adapt to stress and maintain resilience, with sleep doing most of the heavy lifting. Strong recovery supports energy, mood, cognition, immunity and long-term health because sleep and circadian stability regulate repair, hormones and nervous-system recovery.

Recovery is driven primarily by sleep duration, sleep quality and circadian habits. This section focuses on the highest-value behaviours first, so the biggest contributors to daily energy, training response and broader health are given the most weight.

### Sleep (95% of Recovery)

> **Group info tip:** Sleep is the main driver of recovery because this is when the body performs most of its repair, hormonal regulation, nervous-system recovery and memory consolidation. Better sleep improves energy, mood, immunity, training adaptation and long-term health.

| Factor | Relative Weight | Optimal | 80/20 | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sleep duration / quality | 58% | 7–9 hours of uninterrupted, refreshing sleep | 6.5–7 hours, generally good continuity, most nights feeling reasonably refreshed | Sleep quantity and sleep quality work together: the main benefit comes from getting enough total sleep and sleeping well enough that it is actually restorative. |
| Morning light exposure | 11% | 10–15 min outdoors within 1 hour of waking | 5 min within 2–3 hours of waking | Morning light helps anchor circadian rhythm, improving daytime alertness and making sleep onset easier later. |
| Alcohol timing | 7% | Avoid alcohol within 3–4 hours of bed | Occasional moderate intake ≥2 hours before bed | Alcohol can fragment sleep and reduce sleep quality even if it initially makes you feel sleepy. |
| Consistent wake time | 7% | Wake time consistent within ±15 minutes daily | Wake time within ±30 minutes | A stable wake time helps set the sleep-wake cycle and often improves sleep quality more reliably than chasing a perfect bedtime alone. |
| Caffeine timing | 6% | Avoid high-dose caffeine within 10–12 hours of bed | Moderate caffeine allowed 4–6 hours before bed | Late caffeine can reduce sleep depth and delay sleep onset even when you still feel tired. |
| Evening light / device reduction | 4% | Dim lights and avoid screens 60–90 min before bed | Reduce brightness/screens 30–60 min before bed | Reducing evening light and device exposure lowers circadian disruption and can make winding down easier. |
| Stress management before bed | 4% | Low stress with deliberate wind-down practice | 5–10 minutes of calming activity | Lower pre-sleep stress reduces arousal and makes it easier to fall asleep and stay asleep. |
| Room temperature | 1% | 17–20°C (63–68°F) | 20–22°C acceptable | A cooler room supports the drop in body temperature that helps sleep onset and sleep maintenance. |
| Bedroom darkness | 1% | Pitch-black bedroom | Minimal light acceptable | A darker sleep environment reduces disruption and supports healthy circadian signalling. |

### Active Recovery (5% of Recovery)

> **Group info tip:** Active recovery includes low-fatigue habits around training that can modestly improve how well you recover between sessions. These behaviours may help, but they matter far less than sleep.

| Factor | Relative Weight | Optimal | 80/20 | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Post-workout active recovery | 60% | 10–15 min easy walking/cycling/mobility after each session | 5–10 min after ~70–80% of sessions | Easy movement after training may reduce stiffness, maintain circulation and help you feel fresher for the next session. |
| Post-exercise nutrition / protein timing | 40% | 20–30g protein within 30–60 min post-exercise (+ carbs after long/hard cardio) | 15–20g protein within ~2 hours after most sessions | Post-exercise nutrition supports muscle repair and helps replenish energy stores, especially when training volume is higher. |

## Nutrition

> **Info tip:** Nutrition covers the quality, quantity and pattern of food and fluid intake that support health, body composition, energy stability and long-term disease risk. This section prioritises the broad dietary behaviours with the strongest practical evidence rather than niche rules, supplement marketing or influencer-driven food anxiety.

Nutrition here is framed around the fundamentals that move both vitality and long-term health markers most: energy balance, food quality, fibre, protein, alcohol, and basic dietary pattern. The goal is practical, defensible guidance rather than nutritional perfectionism.

| Factor | Weight | Optimal | 80/20 | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Calories / energy intake | 24% | Eat within ±10% of maintenance or goal | Roughly align intake with body-weight goals; avoid chronic overeating | Energy intake remains the main driver of body-weight change and strongly affects energy balance, recovery and long-term adherence. |
| Limit ultra-processed foods | 18% | Diet primarily minimally processed; UPFs rarely consumed | Keep UPFs to a minority of intake (~<25–30% of calories) | Keeping ultra-processed foods low generally improves diet quality, appetite regulation and the odds of meeting nutrition needs without excess calories. |
| Dietary fiber intake | 16% | ≥30 g/day from whole foods (vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains) | ~20–25 g/day | Higher fiber intake supports gut health, satiety, blood sugar control and overall dietary quality, especially when it comes from whole foods. |
| Protein intake | 15% | 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day | ~1.2–1.6 g/kg/day, or protein at most meals | Adequate protein supports muscle repair, recovery, satiety and body composition, especially when training or dieting. |
| Alcohol intake | 12% | Preferably none | Within low-risk guidelines (≤1 drink/day average, no binge drinking) | Alcohol adds little nutritional value and can impair sleep, recovery, appetite regulation and long-term health when intake rises. |
| Processed meat limitation | 7% | Avoid or keep very minimal (<1 serving/week) | Occasional consumption only | Keeping processed meat intake very low supports overall dietary quality and aligns with lower long-term health risk. |
| Micronutrient status | 5% | Meet recommended daily intakes across vitamins/minerals | Varied diet with vegetables, fruit, whole grains, protein sources daily | Micronutrient coverage supports immune function, recovery and general health, and dietary variety is the most practical way to cover the basics. |
| Hydration | 2% | ~2–3 L total fluids/day (varies with climate/activity) | Drink regularly; avoid persistent thirst | Hydration supports concentration, physical performance, digestion and day-to-day function, but exact needs vary with climate, body size and activity. |
| Meal timing / distribution | 1% | Distribute protein and energy across 3–4 meals | Avoid very large single meals; protein across ≥2 meals | Meal distribution matters less than core intake quality, but a more even pattern can support steadier energy, appetite control and practical protein intake. |

## Activity

> **Info tip:** Activity covers the major forms of movement that support health and physical capacity, including resistance training, cardio, daily movement and functional work. It improves cardiovascular fitness, strength, metabolic health, mobility, bone health and long-term independence because repeated physical demand drives adaptation across the body.

This section balances fitness, function and health protection. It gives weight to the exercise patterns most consistently associated with strength, aerobic capacity, lower sedentary time and long-term physical resilience, without pretending every small training detail matters equally.

### Resistance Training (47% of Activity)

> **Group info tip:** Resistance training covers exercise against load, such as weights, machines or progressively harder bodyweight work. It supports strength, muscle mass, bone density and long-term physical capacity because repeated tension drives muscular and neural adaptation.

| Factor | Relative Weight | Optimal | 80/20 | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Weekly resistance training volume | 38% | 60–120 min/week across 3–4 sessions | 30–60 min/week across 2–3 sessions | Most resistance-training benefit comes from doing enough weekly work consistently, not from any single perfect workout. |
| Training intensity | 21% | 70–85% 1RM for most working sets | 60–75% 1RM | Moderate-to-heavy loading supports strength and muscle gains, while slightly lighter work still covers most of the benefit. |
| Progressive overload | 19% | Increase load or total volume weekly | Increase load or reps every 2–3 weeks | Progressive overload is what keeps resistance training effective over time rather than merely repetitive. |
| Power / explosiveness | 13% | 1–3 sessions/week including jumps, throws, or fast lifts | 1 session/week | Power work supports athleticism, coordination and healthy ageing, but matters less than consistently covering the basics. |
| Strength-specific movements | 9% | 3–5 sets of compound lifts per session | 2–3 sets per session | Compound strength work adds extra benefit for strength and bone-loading on top of general resistance training. |

### Cardio (26% of Activity)

> **Group info tip:** Cardio covers sustained and interval-based work that challenges the heart, lungs and aerobic energy systems. It improves cardiovascular fitness, work capacity and metabolic health because regular aerobic demand strengthens oxygen delivery and use.

| Factor | Relative Weight | Optimal | 80/20 | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Endurance / steady-state cardio | 62% | 150–300 min/week moderate cardio (~7,000–10,000 steps/day equivalent) | 75–150 min/week moderate cardio (~5,000–7,000 steps/day equivalent) | Steady-state movement is a major driver of cardiovascular health, stamina and baseline daily activity. |
| HIIT / vigorous cardio | 38% | 2–3 sessions/week interval work (~20–30 min) | 1–2 sessions/week | Vigorous work is efficient for improving fitness, but it creates more fatigue and does not need to dominate the programme. |

### Functional Movement (17% of Activity)

> **Group info tip:** Functional movement includes mobility, flexibility, balance and coordination work that supports movement quality and healthy ageing. It improves stability, range of motion and confidence in real-world movement because repeated practice refines motor control and tissue tolerance.

| Factor | Relative Weight | Optimal | 80/20 | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flexibility / mobility | 59% | 10–15 min most days | 5–10 min most days | Regular mobility work supports range of motion, movement quality and long-term movement resilience. |
| Balance / coordination / skill work | 41% | 2–3 sessions/week | 1 session/week | Balance, coordination and skill work support stability, control and long-term functional fitness. |

### Lifestyle Activity (10% of Activity)

> **Group info tip:** Lifestyle activity covers how much you move outside formal exercise. Reducing sedentary time helps support cardiometabolic health, energy expenditure and general physical function.

| Factor | Relative Weight | Optimal | 80/20 | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sedentary time | 100% | <7 hours/day sitting; frequent movement breaks | <9 hours/day sitting | Long uninterrupted sitting is associated with worse health outcomes, so regular movement through the day matters even if you exercise. |

Mental health is treated here as a major health domain, not an optional extra. This section focuses on practical, non-clinical factors that influence stress load, resilience, social wellbeing and day-to-day functioning.

## How the Scoring Works

Each tab contains either a set of key behaviors or grouped sub-categories. Every item has a weight reflecting its relative contribution within its category, based on practical importance, not trendiness. The app provides two reference points for self-assessment:

- **Optimal** — a strong evidence-based target associated with better health, function, or resilience.
- **80/20** — a simpler, more achievable standard that still captures much of the benefit for most people.

Enter a score from 0–100 for each behavior based on how closely current habits match the guidance. The app multiplies your score by each item's weight, totals the results for the category, and combines category scores into the overall score. Use the overall tab to identify the changes most likely to improve both daily vitality and broader health outcomes first.
